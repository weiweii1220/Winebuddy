How to taste wine +

Look at the color, give the glass a gentle swirl, smell, then take a sip. Notice what you enjoy: fruit, freshness, softness, or richness. You don’t need to identify every aroma for your opinion to count.

What does “dry” mean? +

In wine, “dry” usually means there is little noticeable sweetness. It describes the wine’s taste, not the drying sensation from tannins. If you prefer a hint of sweetness, try an off-dry style.

Pairing wine with food +

Start by matching the weight of the wine to the weight of the dish, or choose a wine with enough freshness to balance rich food. Most importantly, pair with what you like — there are no strict rules at your table.

01FOUNDATIONSWhat Is Wine?Grapes, yeast and a little transformation.+

Wine is made when yeast turns the natural sugars in grape juice into alcohol and carbon dioxide. This process is called fermentation. A winemaker may then blend, age, filter or bottle the wine in different ways.

Wine grapes are varieties selected for winemaking; their skins, seeds and ripeness all influence the juice and eventual style. A wine's flavour also depends on growing conditions, harvest, fermentation and choices in the cellar. A grape name is a useful clue, never a promise that every bottle will taste alike.

Try this: When you taste, notice one thing you smell, one thing you feel (such as freshness or weight), and whether you enjoy it.

Common mix-up: Wine is not simply grape juice with alcohol added; fermentation is the central transformation, though some wine styles involve additional steps.

Takeaway: Start with what you notice and enjoy; expert vocabulary is optional.

Further reading: WSET on tasting and training your palate.

Next: Wine styles · Fermentation in the glossary

02FOUNDATIONSRed, White, Rosé and Sparkling Wine ExplainedColour and bubbles are about process as well as grapes.+

Red wine usually gets its colour and much of its tannin from contact between juice and dark grape skins during fermentation. White wine is commonly made with less skin contact, including from dark-skinned grapes. Rosé gets a shorter period of skin contact or another permitted method, so its shade and character vary.

Sparkling wine contains carbon dioxide that creates bubbles. Some sparkling wines gain bubbles through a second fermentation in the bottle; others use a tank method or other permitted approaches. These methods can affect texture and aroma, but the label and producer's notes are better guides than a single rule.

Example: Champagne is a protected regional name for sparkling wine from Champagne, France; sparkling wine from elsewhere uses other names. Common mix-up: Rosé is not necessarily sweet, and sparkling is not necessarily dry.

Takeaway: Colour does not settle sweetness. Check the style description and label cues.

Further reading: Comité Champagne on dosage and sweetness styles.

Related: Dry vs. sweet · Six grapes to know

03FOUNDATIONSDry vs. Sweet WineSweetness is not the same as fruitiness or dryness on your lips.+

In wine, sweetness mainly refers to residual sugar: grape sugar left after fermentation. A wine described as dry has little remaining sugar by the relevant style or regulatory definition. Wines can also be off-dry or sweet. Exact category definitions depend on the region and wine type.

Fruitiness means fruit-like aromas or flavours. A dry wine can smell of ripe peach or berries; a sweet wine can still have high acidity. Acidity can make a wine feel lively and can balance sweetness, so it is not a measure of sugar or dryness.

Label clue: For Champagne, Brut means less than 12 grams of sugar per litre under the regional classification; other regions and styles may use different terms or rules. Common mix-up: tannin can make gums feel dry, but that sensation is not residual sugar.

Try this: If you prefer a little sweetness, look for a clearly described off-dry style, then check the producer's description.

Takeaway: Ask separately: does it smell fruity, and does it taste sweet?

Further reading: Comité Champagne sweetness terms · WSET on acidity and sweetness.

04FOUNDATIONSBody, Acidity and Tannins ExplainedThree different parts of how wine feels.+

Body is the overall weight or fullness in the mouth. Alcohol, extract, sugar and other factors can influence that impression; body is not a synonym for alcohol, richness or tannin.

Acidity often brings freshness and a mouth-watering sensation. It exists in dry and sweet wines alike. Tannin is a drying or grippy sensation, especially noticeable in many red wines; it can come from grape skins, seeds, stems or oak. Tannin is not the same as bitterness, though they may be noticed together.

Simple tasting check: Does it feel light or weighty? Does your mouth water? Do your gums feel grippy? These are separate observations and can all be present at once.

Common mix-up: A full-bodied wine is not automatically high in tannin; a light-bodied wine can still have lively acidity.

Takeaway: Describe body, acidity and tannin separately instead of compressing them into “strong.”

Further reading: WSET introduction to wine types, styles and body · WSET on acidity.

05FOUNDATIONSSix Grape Varieties Beginners Should KnowUseful reference points, with room for variation.+
  • Cabernet Sauvignon: often structured, with dark-fruit notes and noticeable tannin, though climate and winemaking matter.
  • Pinot Noir: often lighter in colour and body, with red-fruit notes; examples range widely.
  • Merlot: commonly associated with plum-like fruit and a softer impression than some Cabernet-led wines, but not always.
  • Chardonnay: can range from lean and citrus-led to fuller, including examples influenced by malolactic fermentation or oak.
  • Sauvignon Blanc: often fresh and aromatic, with citrus or herbal notes that depend on growing conditions and style.
  • Riesling: can be dry through sweet; its acidity and aromas vary with region, ripeness and producer.

Try this: Pick one grape and compare two bottles from different places or producers when convenient. Notice what repeats and what changes.

Common mix-up: The grape alone does not guarantee sweetness, flavour, body or quality. Regional labels may name a place rather than a grape.

Takeaway: Grape names are starting clues, not a tasting guarantee.

Further reading: WSET on using grape varieties as clues · Rioja's official grape-variety guide.

06CHOOSING WINEUnderstanding Wine Regions and LabelsLearn which clues are useful and which need context.+

A label may foreground the producer, grape variety, region, or a named appellation (a legally defined place with its own rules). “Varietal” generally means a wine identified by a grape variety. The permitted grape and vintage-label rules vary by country and region, so do not assume one global standard.

Vintage means the harvest year shown for a wine when applicable; it does not simply mean “old.” Some wines blend grapes from more than one year and may not display a vintage. An appellation can tell you where grapes were grown and which regional rules apply, but it cannot guarantee that you will like the wine.

Example: Rioja is a region and protected designation in Spain, while Tempranillo is an important grape variety used there. Common mix-up: Old World/New World are broad, informal shorthand, not quality grades or precise geographic laws.

Practical tip: Find the producer and region first; use a trusted regional guide or back label to decode unfamiliar terms.

Takeaway: A label offers clues whose meaning depends on local rules.

Further reading: WSET on wine-label clues · EU wine labelling rules (one jurisdiction's example) · Rioja grape varieties.

Related: Appellation · Choosing a first bottle

07CHOOSING WINEHow to Choose Your First Bottle of WineStart with a preference, not a test of expertise.+

Start with the occasion, food and a taste you already enjoy. If you like crisp drinks, explore fresh whites or sparkling styles. If you like ripe fruit, ask about fruit-forward wines; if you want obvious sweetness, say so separately. If you prefer less grip, ask about lower-tannin reds or choose a white or rosé.

Use the grape or region as a clue, then read the producer's description. Ask a shop professional for a style recommendation within a budget you set. Compare the actual shelf prices yourself; WineBuddy does not check them.

Useful question: “I’d like a dry, lighter-bodied red with less tannin for pasta. What styles fit my budget?” Replace those preferences with your own.

Common mix-up: A higher price is not a reliable shortcut to personal enjoyment. Labels and grapes are clues, not promises.

Takeaway: State one thing you like, one thing you want to avoid, and your budget.

08CHOOSING WINEHow to Choose Wine on a BudgetUse a spending limit and compare like with like.+

Set a maximum spend that feels comfortable before browsing. Prices vary by country, tax, retailer, vintage, producer and supply, so a style name cannot tell you the current local price. Compare actual bottles at your shop, including the size and any delivery or service costs.

Try a region or grape that is new to you, ask for a similar style at a lower price, or consider a smaller format if available. These are ways to explore, not guarantees of value. If you already know a style you enjoy, ask for a comparable option rather than choosing from a prestige word alone.

Practical tip: Tell a shop professional your budget ceiling plainly. They can suggest options currently available there.

Common mix-up: “Affordable” is relative, and WineBuddy has no live prices, retailer listings, ratings or stock data.

Takeaway: Let your own budget set the limit; verify each price where you shop.

09ENJOYING WINEA Beginner's Guide to Food PairingFlexible ideas to help the food and wine work together.+

Pairing is about how food changes the way wine tastes, and vice versa. A fresh, acidic wine can feel refreshing with rich food; salty or fatty elements can soften the impression of some tannic reds. Food with noticeable sweetness can make a dry wine seem less fruity or more bitter, so a wine at least as sweet as a dessert is often a useful starting point.

For spicy food, lower-alcohol, fresh or slightly sweet styles may feel gentler to some palates; strong alcohol and tannin can feel more pronounced. These are tendencies, not rules: spice intensity, sauce, preparation and personal preference all matter.

Example: Try a crisp white with a lemony vegetable dish, or a fruitier red with pizza. Notice which component works for you.

Common mix-up: “Red with meat, white with fish” is not a law. Sauce and seasoning can matter as much as the main ingredient.

Takeaway: Match intensity, consider sweetness and seasoning, and trust your own preference.

Further reading: WSET on flexible food and wine pairing · WSET on spice and drink style.

10ENJOYING WINEServing, Storing and Tasting WineSimple steps; no special equipment required.+

Serve wine in a clean glass that lets you smell it. White and sparkling wines are often served chilled; many reds show better slightly cool than warm room temperature. Exact preferences vary, and chilling a red briefly is fine if that is how you enjoy it. Champagne's regional body suggests roughly 8–10°C for Champagne specifically; this is not a universal rule for all wine.

Store unopened wine away from heat, strong light and large temperature swings. A cool, stable place is more useful than a complicated gadget. Once opened, recork it and refrigerate; use your senses and the producer's advice, because how long it remains enjoyable varies by style and handling.

To taste, look, smell, sip and notice the finish (the flavours and sensations that remain after swallowing or spitting). Swirling is optional. You never need to finish a glass or bottle to learn.

Common mix-up: Decanting is not required for every red; it can aerate some wines or separate sediment, but older or delicate wines may lose aroma quickly.

Takeaway: Keep it cool, safe and comfortable; your own observations are enough.

Further reading: Comité Champagne serving and storage guidance.

Acidity
A wine component that can create freshness and a mouth-watering sensation. It is separate from sweetness. Lesson 4
Aroma
A smell perceived from wine, often used for the scents noticed before tasting. Aroma is not a promise of literal ingredients. Lesson 1
Body
The overall impression of weight or fullness in the mouth; it is distinct from tannin and not determined by alcohol alone. Lesson 4
Brut
A regulated sweetness term on sparkling wine labels. For Champagne, Brut means less than 12 g/L residual sugar; rules vary by category and region. Lesson 3
Dry
Wine with little residual sugar under the relevant style definition. It does not mean tannic, acidic, or physically drying. Lesson 3
Tannin
Compounds that can create a drying or grippy mouthfeel. In wine they may come from grape parts or oak; they are distinct from body. Lesson 4
Vintage
The harvest year shown for a wine when applicable. It is not a synonym for old or high quality; labelling rules differ. Lesson 6
Terroir
A broad French term for the natural and human factors connected with a place that influence growing and wine style. Its exact use can vary. Lesson 6
Varietal
A wine identified by the grape variety used. Labelling thresholds and rules differ by country; the word alone does not ensure a single-grape wine everywhere. Lesson 5
Finish
The flavours and sensations that remain after a sip. People may describe a finish by its length, texture or character. Lesson 10
Oak
Wood used for some wine vessels. Oak can affect oxygen exposure and may contribute aromas or texture; its impact depends on vessel, age and use. Lesson 5
Sweetness
The taste impression associated mainly with residual sugar, influenced by balance with acidity and other components. It is not the same as fruitiness. Lesson 3
Alcohol by volume (ABV)
The percentage of a drink's volume that is pure alcohol. It is shown on labels in many jurisdictions; alcohol can affect perceived warmth and body. Lesson 4
Decanting
Pouring wine from its bottle into another vessel, sometimes to separate sediment or expose it to air. It is optional and effects vary. Lesson 10
Fermentation
The process in which yeast converts grape sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide, though methods and outcomes vary. Lesson 1
Blend
A wine made by combining different grape varieties, vineyard lots, or wines, depending on the producer's choices and regional rules. Lesson 6
Residual sugar
Sugars in finished wine, commonly those left unfermented; some styles also receive permitted sweetening. Sugar is one factor in perceived sweetness. Lesson 3
Corked wine
Wine affected by cork taint, often associated with a musty or damp-cardboard smell that can mute fruit. It does not simply mean a bottle closed with cork. Lesson 10
Old World wine
Informal shorthand often referring to traditional European wine-producing regions. Boundaries and implications are imprecise; it is not a quality grade. Lesson 6
New World wine
Informal shorthand often referring to regions outside traditional European wine countries. It is not a strict category or quality ranking. Lesson 6
Appellation
A defined geographic wine area with rules about production; the specific legal meaning varies by jurisdiction. Lesson 6
Bouquet
A term sometimes used for aromas that develop through fermentation, ageing or bottle maturation. Usage varies; “aroma” is also common. Lesson 1
Sparkling wine
Wine containing dissolved carbon dioxide that produces bubbles when opened. Styles and production methods vary. Lesson 2
Fortified wine
Wine to which distilled spirit, usually grape spirit, has been added during or after fermentation. Styles differ in sweetness and method. Lesson 2
Pairing
Choosing food and wine together, considering how their flavours and structures interact. There are helpful principles, not universal rules. Lesson 9
Rosé
Pink wine made using methods that limit or manage colour extraction from dark grapes. It can be dry or sweet. Lesson 2
Malolactic fermentation
A bacterial conversion that changes sharper malic acid to softer lactic acid. It can influence texture and flavour, especially in some white wines. Lesson 5
Vintage variation
Differences between harvest years that can affect grape ripeness and wine style; their impact depends on region, producer and wine. Lesson 6
Traditional method
A sparkling-wine method in which the second fermentation that creates bubbles takes place in the bottle. Terminology varies by region. Lesson 2
